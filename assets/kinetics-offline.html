Consecutive reactions: the rise and fall of an intermediate

A first forms B, which then forms C. B is an intermediate: it builds up while its formation is faster than its consumption, then falls as A runs out. With both preset rate constants equal to 1 s−1, B reaches its maximum at 1 s and C eventually accumulates.

Try increasing the second rate constant to make B shorter-lived, or decreasing it to let B accumulate. Compare the height and timing of the B peak. The total [A] + [B] + [C] stays constant in this model.

Reversible reactions: a dynamic equilibrium

A and B continually interconvert. At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates balance, so the concentrations stop changing even though both reactions continue. The preset starts with A = 1 mol/L and B = 0; equal rate constants lead to 0.5 mol/L of each.

Try doubling the forward rate constant: the final ratio [B]/[A] approaches kforward/kreverse = 2. Alternatively, multiply both constants by ten: the same equilibrium is reached more quickly.

Parallel reactions: competing pathways

A can become either B or C. Both pathways consume the same starting material, and their relative rate constants determine how it is divided between the products. With the two preset constants equal, B and C approach equal final concentrations.

Try changing the rate constant for A → C from 1 to 3 s−1. Of the A that reacts, one quarter becomes B and three quarters become C. A disappears with a combined first-order rate constant equal to the sum of the two pathway constants.

Enzyme kinetics: binding, turnover, and recycling

Free enzyme E binds substrate S to form ES. The complex can release S again or form product P and regenerate E. The solver follows all three elementary steps directly. It does not impose the steady-state approximation used to derive the familiar Michaelis–Menten rate equation.

Try lowering the initial enzyme concentration from 1 to 0.01 mol/L while keeping S at 1 mol/L, and extend the time interval to see product build up. Watch ES form and E return as substrate is depleted. The totals [E] + [ES] and [S] + [ES] + [P] remain constant. The preset concentrations are illustrative teaching values.

Dimerization: two reactants make one product

Two A molecules combine to make one B. The elementary reaction rate is v = k[A]2, and A disappears at twice that rate: −d[A]/dt = 2k[A]2. This factor of two is included automatically from the mechanism.

With the preset k = 1 L mol−1 s−1 and [A]0 = 1 mol/L, half of A remains at 0.5 s. Try doubling [A]0: the initial rate increases fourfold and the half-life halves. The conserved quantity is [A] + 2[B], because each B contains two A units.

Fast and slow reactions: two very different time scales

A becomes B rapidly, then B converts to C more slowly. The preset constants are 10,000 and 1 s−1: the first step acts on a roughly 0.0001 s time scale, while the second takes about 1 s. Such widely separated time scales make this a stiff kinetic problem.

Try setting the end time to 0.002 s to resolve the initial rise of B, then return to 10 s to follow its slower decay. The solver adapts its internal steps to the dynamics. Output points control the sampled curve; a coarse plot can miss a rapid feature even when the integration resolves it.

Chiral amplification · Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2026

This simplified Frank-type teaching model illustrates asymmetric autocatalysis and mutual inhibition, ideas connected to the 2026 Chemistry Nobel awarded to Henri B. Kagan and Kenso Soai. A feeds the growth of either enantiomer, R or S; each catalyzes its own formation, while R + S forms inactive D. Official prize background · Frank’s 1953 paper.

The preset starts with a tiny R excess. The deterministic equations preserve an exactly racemic state; no random fluctuations are added. Try setting both initial concentrations to 0.1, or swap R = 0.10001 and S = 0.1 to reverse the favored hand. These are illustrative classroom parameters, not measured conditions for the Soai reaction.

Lotka–Volterra: predator–prey cycles

Prey reproduce, encounters convert prey into predators, and predators die when food is scarce. This reaction-like notation generates the classical predator–prey equations. The preset uses scaled populations and time, with all three rate parameters equal to one.

Open the phase-plane view to follow Predator versus Prey. Try changing the initial prey population from 1.5 to 2: the orbit changes. These ideal closed orbits are neutrally stable, not an attracting limit cycle. The equilibrium is Prey = Predator = 1; starting exactly there produces a steady state.

The Brusselator: a stable limit-cycle attractor

A constant source feeds X; conversion to Y competes with the autocatalytic step 2 X + Y → 3 X. Fixed external reservoirs are absorbed into the effective rates. This idealized, scaled mass-action model can settle onto a repeating orbit after its initial transient.

The preset has feed parameter A = 1 and conversion parameter B = 3. With this rate convention, the steady state loses stability when B > 1 + A². Try lowering the rate of X → Y from 3 to 1.5: oscillations decay toward a steady state. Restore 3 and vary the initial concentrations to see trajectories approach the same limit cycle.

The Oregonator: a model of the BZ reaction

The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction couples autocatalysis to delayed inhibition. This three-variable, f = 1 Oregonator skeleton tracks an activator X, an inhibitor Y, and an oxidized-catalyst variable Z. Constant feed reservoirs are folded into the effective rate constants; untracked products are represented by 0.

Watch repeated pulses after the initial transient, then choose X and Z in the phase-plane view and hide the first half of the run. Try changing the rate of Z → Y to alter the feedback timing, or changing the initial conditions. These are illustrative scaled parameters for a well-mixed kinetic model, not a laboratory recipe or a simulation of spatial spiral waves. A periodic limit cycle is not a chaotic attractor.

How the model works

For each elementary step, the rate is the rate constant multiplied by the reactant concentrations raised to their stoichiometric powers. For 2 A -> B, the reaction rate is v = k[A]2, so d[A]/dt = −2v and d[B]/dt = v. This convention matters when entering a measured rate constant.

The model assumes a well-mixed system at constant volume and fixed rate constants. It does not infer rate laws for overall reactions, check elemental balance, or include diffusion, temperature changes, or stochastic effects. For physical concentration models, use consistent seconds and mol/L units; a reaction of total order n needs k in (mol/L)1−n s−1.

Write a reversible step with <-> to get separate forward and reverse constants. Repeated reactants are combined: A + A means 2 A. Use 0 -> A for a constant source and A -> 0 for a first-order sink. Lines can contain # comments. Charged species should use labels such as H_plus; the plus sign separates reactants. Limits: 32 species, 80 one-way steps, and 2,001 output points.

Numerical results are approximate. A calculation that cannot meet the tolerance or reaches a time limit stops with a message instead of displaying an incomplete result. The concentration sum is not generally conserved: for dimerization, for example, [A] + 2[B] is the conserved quantity.
