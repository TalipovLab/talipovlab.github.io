Getting started in research

Choosing a project, working with a mentor, and planning your next step.

Choose a question and a workable commitment

Undergraduate research offers practice asking questions whose answers are not yet known. Projects can involve experiments, computation, theory, data analysis, or a combination. Read a group’s current work and identify a question or method you would like to learn.

  • Contact a potential mentor with a short introduction, your interests, relevant coursework or experience, and your realistic availability. You do not need to claim expertise you have not yet developed.
  • Discuss what a beginning project could involve, who would supervise day to day, and how progress and feedback would be handled.
  • Clarify expected hours, scheduling, training, credit or pay, and any funding conditions before committing. Research participation does not itself guarantee a paid position.
  • Ask about accessibility needs and the tools or facilities required. For computational work, agree on account access, software, data storage, and how to obtain help.

What to expect

  • Research is iterative. You may revise the question, repeat a calculation or experiment, and revisit an interpretation after new evidence appears.
  • Keep a record of methods, input files or procedures, software versions, results, and unsuccessful attempts. Make your work understandable to your future self and your collaborators.
  • Bring questions and intermediate results to meetings. Separate what you observed from what you infer, and discuss uncertainty openly.
  • Agree on data stewardship, collaboration, and authorship expectations early, then revisit them as the project develops.
  • If a project or mentoring arrangement is not working, discuss possible changes with the mentor or an appropriate departmental adviser. Concerns about safety or respectful treatment should be raised promptly.

Plan your next step

Research can help you evaluate graduate study, scientific employment, teaching, and other careers. Degree and training timelines vary by program and career; a doctorate or postdoctoral appointment is not required for every scientific role.

For summer programs, begin checking opportunities in the preceding fall. Deadlines, eligibility, required recommendations, and financial support differ by program. Give recommenders adequate notice and provide the information they request.
