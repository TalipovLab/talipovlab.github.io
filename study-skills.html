Teaching & learning

How to study chemistry

Practice, feedback, and a manageable routine for learning chemical ideas.

A repeatable study routine

Understanding chemistry means connecting observations, molecular pictures, equations, and quantities. Use the course learning objectives to decide what to practice, and the syllabus to check assessment rules.

  • Prepare a question. Before class, scan the assigned material and identify something you cannot yet explain. During class, record the reasoning and assumptions behind an example, not just its final answer.
  • Try before looking. Close the worked solution and attempt a problem or explain a concept from memory. If you need a hint, use it, then start again without it.
  • Check and diagnose. Compare your approach with a reliable solution. Record whether the difficulty was a concept, an equation choice, units, algebra, or interpretation. Correct the reasoning as well as the number.
  • Return after a delay. Revisit the idea on later days and mix it with earlier topics. Adjust the spacing and difficulty to what you can retrieve; there is no universal number of study hours that guarantees a grade.
  • Explain the result. Say what the answer means chemically. Check units, sign, magnitude, and a limiting case. Can you translate between a graph, a molecular picture, and an equation?

Practice chemical reasoning

  • Quantitative problems: list known quantities with units, define the unknown, and state the physical or chemical model before substituting numbers. Keep extra digits during the calculation and round appropriately at the end.
  • Structures and bonding: practice formal charge, electron counting, and the limitations of the model being used. The octet rule is a useful introductory pattern, not a universal law.
  • Equilibrium and kinetics: distinguish how far a reaction proceeds from how fast it proceeds. In an equilibrium expression, use activities or the approximations appropriate to the course; a large equilibrium constant does not imply a fast reaction.
  • Before an assessment: use instructor-authorized practice questions under realistic conditions. Check which notes, calculators, collaborators, and other tools are allowed.

Get feedback early

Bring a specific attempt and a specific question to office hours or a study group. Take turns explaining solutions, then solve a related problem independently. Asking for help is a way to identify gaps and plan the next step.

Follow your instructor’s rules for collaboration and AI tools. Verify any generated explanation, calculation, or citation against course materials and your own reasoning; do not submit assistance as independent work when it is not permitted.

Laboratory work

Read the procedure before starting, understand the purpose of each measurement, and keep a clear record of observations and units. Follow the course or laboratory’s required training, protective equipment, and waste procedures. Ask the instructor or supervisor before proceeding when a safety instruction is unclear.

Why retrieval and spacing?

Experiments support using retrieval practice and returning to material over time to improve longer-term retention. The best schedule depends on the material, learner, and retention goal; these findings do not promise a particular course grade.

  • Roediger & Karpicke (2006): test-enhanced learning
  • Cepeda and colleagues (2008): spacing and retention
